1.1
Preamble
McArthur River mine production began in the fourth quarter of 1999 and entered commercial production November 1, 2000. In January 2018, production
activities were suspended at McArthur River mine and Key Lake mill for approximately four years due to continued weakness in the global uranium market. In February 2022, as market conditions improved and long-term contracting activity increased
significantly, Cameco and its joint venture partner, Orano Canada Inc. (Orano) announced the planned restart of both operations. Following the completion of automation upgrades and restart preparations, the operations were officially restarted in
November 2022.
This technical report supersedes the McArthur River Operation technical report filed in March of 2019 (2018 Technical Report). It
incorporates updated technical and scientific information, and reflects operational knowledge and experience gained since the restart of operations.

1.4
Location and site description
The McArthur River mine is a fully developed property located near Toby Lake in northern Saskatchewan, approximately 620 kilometres north of
Saskatoon. The McArthur River mine site is compact, with the principal mine surface facilities occupying an area of approximately one km
2
, excluding the nearby airstrip and camp facilities.
The McArthur River mine site contains all the services and infrastructure to support a remote underground mining operation. Site infrastructure includes
a 1.6 kilometre long gravel airstrip and air terminal, permanent camp facilities, administration and maintenance buildings, warehouse facilities, water containment ponds and a water treatment plant, freeze plant, concrete batch plant, one
full-service shaft and two ventilation shafts, site roads, electrical substations and backup generators, an ore slurry load out facility, freshwater pump house and water distribution systems plus other ancillary infrastructure and support buildings.
Access to the McArthur River property is by an
all-weather
road and by air. Supplies are transported to the
site by truck on a year-round basis and can be shipped from locations throughout North America through Cameco’s transit warehouse in Saskatoon. An
80-kilometre
gravel road connects the mine site and the
Key Lake Operation. Calcined uranium ore concentrate is shipped year-round by truck from the Key Lake Operation to Saskatoon and other destinations. The unpaved airstrip and terminal are located approximately one kilometre east of the mine site
within the surface lease and provide air access to and from the McArthur River property.
The regional topography and the environmental setting are
typical of the taiga forested terrain of the Athabasca Basin area of northern Saskatchewan. Surface facilities are at an elevation of approximately 550 metres above sea level.
McArthur River has sufficient surface rights to meet all its current mining operation needs. No tailings management facilities are required as McArthur
River mineralization is sent to the Key Lake mill for processing.

1.5
Geology and mineralization
The McArthur River uranium deposit is located approximately 40 km west of the eastern margin of the Athabasca Basin of northern Saskatchewan (
Figure
7-1
). Like other major uranium deposits of the basin, it is located at or near the unconformity contact separating late Paleoproterozoic sandstone of the basal portion of the Athabasca Group from
Paleoproterozoic metasedimentary gneisses and migmatites of the Wollaston Group which are interfolded with Archean granitoid gneisses.
High-grade
uranium mineralization has been delineated from surface drilling over a strike length of 2,700 metres, occurring at depths ranging between 500 to 640 metres below surface. Underground drilling programs have covered approximately 2,300 metres of this
overall strike length.
Mineralized widths are variable along strike, but the most consistent, high-grade mineralization occurs proximal to the main
graphitic reverse fault by the upthrust basement rock. Less consistent and generally lower grade mineralization occurs downdip along this fault contact between basement rock and sandstone. The main part of the mineralization, generally at the upper
part of the basement wedge, averages 12.7 metres in width and has a vertical extent ranging between 50 and 120 metres.
Nine mineralized areas at the
McArthur River deposit have been well defined with underground drilling, namely Zones 1, 2, 3, 4, 4 South, Zone A, Zone B, McA North 1 and McA North 2.
Three other mineralized areas, known as McA North 3, McA North 4 and McA South 1, have not been well defined by surface or underground drilling.
The P2 reverse fault, also known as the P2 structure, is the most important mineralization control for the McArthur River deposit. Mineralization occurs
across a zone of strongly altered basement rocks and sandstone across both the unconformity and the P2 structure. Mineralization is generally within 15 metres of the unconformity contact except for Zone 2. Less significant zones of mineralization
may occur further from the unconformity, usually in the sandstone and are associated with subsidiary fracture/fault zones or along the margins of flat lying siltstone beds.
Zone 2 mineralization occurs deeper in the basement rocks in a unique area of the deposit. Here a footwall quartzite unit lies near the main P2 fault
zone, and high-grade mineralization occurs not only in the hanging wall basement wedge but also overlies the footwall quartzite. Most of this zone has now been mined out.
In general, the high-grade mineralization, characterized by botryoidal uraninite masses and subhedral uraninite aggregates, constitutes the earliest
phase of mineralization in the deposit. Pyrite, chalcopyrite, and galena were also deposited during this initial mineralizing event. Later stage, remobilized uraninite occurs as disseminations, veinlets, and fracture coatings within chlorite breccia
zones and along the margins of silt beds in the Athabasca sandstone and lower quartzite unit.
1.6
Exploration
Cameco, through one of its predecessor companies, the Saskatchewan Mining Development Corporation (SMDC), became operator of the McArthur River project
in 1980. Surface exploration programs, ranging from small line cutting crews to large helicopter supported drilling and prospecting camps, were active from 1980 through to 1992.
In the summer of 1988, drilling was completed along the northern portion of an electromagnetic conductor. The last hole of the year,
MAC-198,
encountered an up thrusted basement/sandstone contact before intersecting a
10-metre-thick
zone of high-grade mineralization. Subsequent surface drilling programs in
1989, 1990, 1991, and 1992 delineated the mineralization over a strike length of 1,700 metres at depths ranging between 500 to 640 metres below surface.
In 1993, an underground exploration program, consisting of shaft sinking, lateral development, and diamond drilling was approved by government agencies.
The first shaft was completed in 1994. Approvals for mine

construction and development were obtained in 1997. Construction and development of the McArthur River mine was completed on schedule and mining commenced in December 1999. Commercial production
was achieved on November 1, 2000.
As of September 30, 2026, 197 surface drillholes totaling more than 121,000 metres, comprising a
combination of conventional and directional diamond drilling, have tested the P2 structure at intervals of 300 metres or less for a distance of 5.0 km northeast and 6.4 km southwest of the mine, respectively. Starting in 2016, exploration efforts
mostly shifted away from the P2 trend to the northern part of the property.
Since 1994, underground drilling programs have covered approximately
2,300 metres of overall strike length, with over 1,500 underground drillholes, totaling more than 141,000 metres, providing detailed delineation information over 1,800 metres of strike length with target spacing of approximately 10 metres on average
between holes. Additionally, over 8,500 additional underground drillholes, totaling more than 471,000 metres, were drilled for geotechnical information; probe and grout covers; service and drain holes; production and freeze holes.

1.8
Mining
The McArthur River deposit presents unique challenges that are not typical of traditional hard or soft rock mines. These challenges are the result of
mining in or near high pressure ground water in challenging ground conditions with significant radiation hazards due to the high-grade uranium mineralization. Mining methods are selected on their ability to mitigate risks associated with these
challenges.
To reduce the inherent risk of an inflow, the high-pressure water sources are isolated from active development and production areas. To
date, McArthur River has relied on pressure grouting and ground freezing to successfully mitigate groundwater risks.
Probe and grout covers are
maintained ahead of the development face to gather geological, geotechnical, hydrological and radiological information. Water intersections are pressure grouted to seal off or restrict water-conductive structures.
Ground freezing is used to isolate entire mine zones from high pressure ground water. To achieve this, a freeze shell is made of interlocking freeze
walls and is anchored into
non-water-bearing
basement rock. Once a freeze wall is established, the area is depressurized and drained. Development and ore extraction can proceed within the inside of the freeze
walls, with a significantly lowered water inflow risk. Ground freezing is also used for ground strengthening in specific areas with very low rock strengths.
There are three approved mining methods at McArthur River: raisebore mining, blasthole stope mining and boxhole mining. However, only raisebore and
blasthole stope mining remain in use.

Raisebore mining
has been in use since 1999. It is suitable for massive high-grade zones where
there is access both above and below the ore zone. The raise opening created by mechanical cutting has an increased stability compared to traditional mining, making this method preferred for mining the weaker rock mass areas of the deposit. In
addition, holes can be drilled accurately over a long distance, eliminating the need for sublevel access. Waste and ore can be easily separated by bucket scanning.
Blasthole Stoping
began in 2011 as a test program and was approved by the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) as an extraction method in
November 2013. Since approval, the use of this method has expanded to the point where most of the ore is now extracted using this method.
Blasthole
stoping is planned in areas where blastholes can be accurately drilled and small stable stopes excavated without jeopardizing the freeze wall integrity. Blasthole stope mining has shown an advantage over raisebore mining on overall extraction
efficiency by reducing underground development, concrete consumption, mineralized waste generation, and improving overall extraction cycle time.
There are currently three active mine zones and one significantly advanced:
Zone 2:
Zone 2 has been actively mined since production began in 1999 and is near completion with 2.1 million pounds of mineral reserves
remaining secured behind freeze walls. The remaining reserves will be recovered with a combination of raisebore and blasthole stope mining.
Zone
4:
Zone 4 has been actively mined since 2010 and has 66.2 million pounds of mineral reserves secured behind freeze walls.
Remaining ore on
the north and south sides of Zone 4 (17.2 million pounds of mineral reserves) where the ground conditions are more favourable will be mined using blasthole stope mining.
The central portion of Zone 4 (49.0 million pounds of mineral reserves), where the ground conditions are notably more challenging due to the
presence of massive clay and highly fractured rock, will be mined by raisebore mining with mass freezing for ground stabilization. Both development and production mining will be slower than other mine areas due to the additional controls and steps
required for safe development and extraction.
Zone 1:
Production began in June 2026 after sufficient development and construction was
completed to allow one production area to be activated. Full development is expected to be complete in 2027. Zone 1 has 46.4 million pounds of mineral reserves secured behind freeze walls. Blasthole stope mining is planned as the main
extraction method.
Zone 4 South:
Zone 4 South is an extension of Zone 4; however, average grades are more than three times lower. The upper
freeze wall cap has been drilled, activated and is currently freezing. Most of the lower freeze wall development has been completed and freeze drilling is actively in progress. Zone 4 South has a mineral reserve of 57.5 million pounds and is
the next zone scheduled to come into production (expected late 2029). Blasthole stope mining is currently planned as the main extraction method.

1.11
Key Lake tailings management
There are two tailings management facilities at the Key Lake Operation:
•
An above ground tailings management facility constructed in 1983, where tailings are stored within compacted till
embankments. Cameco has not deposited tailings here since 1996.
•
The Deilmann tailings management facility (DTMF) was commissioned in 1996 following completion of mining in the eastern
portion of the Deilmann pit in 1995.
Beginning in 2001, periodic sloughing of the western pit walls of the DTMF was observed.
Cameco subsequently implemented the West Wall Stabilization Project, which was completed in 2013, to provide long-term stabilization of the western pit wall and maintain the integrity and performance of the facility. Subsequent slope-remediation
work was completed in the south-side area in 2016 and on the northeast slope in 2019. The DTMF continues to be actively managed and monitored through established geotechnical, water-management and environmental monitoring programs.
The DTMF has sufficient tailings capacity for the reported McArthur River mineral reserves with excess capacity to accommodate conversion of McArthur
River’s mineral resources to mineral reserves and possible future toll-milling opportunities from other mines.

Economic analysis and costs
The economic analysis for the McArthur River Operation is based upon the current mine plan which considers mining and milling the reported mineral
reserves. The analysis does not include any estimates involving the mining and milling of mineral resources.
The analysis is from the point of view
of Cameco, which owns 69.805% of the MRJV, and incorporates a projection of Cameco’s sales revenue from its proportionate share of the related production, less its share of related operating and capital costs of the MRJV, as well as royalties
and surcharges that will be payable on the sale of concentrates.
For the purpose of the economic analysis, the projected impact of income taxes has
been excluded due to the nature of the required calculations. McArthur River operates as an unincorporated joint venture and is, therefore, not subject to direct income taxation at the joint venture level. It is not practical to allocate a resulting
income tax cost to Cameco’s portion of the McArthur River Operation, as Cameco’s tax expense is a function of several variables, most of which are independent of its investment in McArthur River.
The economic analysis resulted in an estimated
pre-tax
NPV (at a discount rate of 8%) to Cameco for net cash
flows from January 1, 2026 forward of $5.86 billion for its share of the current McArthur River mineral reserves.

1.14
Mining and milling risks
The McArthur River/Key Lake operation is subject to mining, milling and infrastructure risks that could adversely affect production, costs and, in some
circumstances, mineral reserve recovery. Principal risks include water inflows, transition delays to new mining areas, geotechnical and mine rehabilitation, productivity performance, plant and system availability, aging infrastructure and
reliability, processing and tailings-system reliability, labour relations and availability of skilled personnel, and long-term power availability. These risks are managed through mine design, engineering and operating controls, monitoring,
improvement initiatives, preventative and predictive maintenance and operational experience; however, they have not been eliminated, and mitigation measures may not be fully effective.

Recommendations
The McArthur River mine/Key Lake mill are mature operations and continue to supply a significant volume of uranium to world markets. To realize the
economic benefits from these operations and to mitigate risk, the authors of this technical report make the following recommendations:
Metallurgical processing:
It is recommended that a mineralogical and metallurgical analysis be completed on future mining areas to validate the
forecast metallurgical and environmental performance assumptions.
Mineral resources and reserves:
The McArthur River Operation estimated
mineral reserves have proven, thus far, to be slightly conservative with more pounds of U
3
O
8
and tonnage extracted than predicted. Over
the years, the mineral reserve model has been calibrated as required to better predict actual production results. Continued review of mineral resource models, mining recovery and dilution modifying factors and monitoring of production data against
model expectations is recommended as additional data is collected.
Given the size of the current proven and probable mineral reserve inventory and
the forecast rate of production, the authors of this technical report are of the opinion that the current exploration plan to delineate Zones A, McA South 1, McA North 3 and McA North 4 cannot be deferred but is sufficient and does not require
acceleration.
Mine recovery:
McArthur River assumes ore recoveries of 97.5% for raisebore mining and 99.0% for drill and blast stope mining
which may be considered high and has not been fully validated against actual recoveries of mined out areas.
It is recommended that recovery
assumptions be further assessed, documented and peer-reviewed plus recommendations made prior to the next
life-of-mine
update. Any potential assumption changes in
recovery are not expected to be significant but may result in a slight reduction in mineral reserves.

5.2
Climate
The McArthur River Operation is located within the continental
sub-arctic

Summers are generally rather short and cool, although the daily temperatures may occasionally exceed 30°C. Mean daily maximum temperatures during the warmest months are approximately 20°C and on average only three months experience mean

Geological setting and mineralization
7.1
Regional geology
The McArthur River uranium deposit is located approximately 40 kilometres west of the eastern margin of the Athabasca Basin of northern Saskatchewan.
Like other major uranium deposits in this area of the basin, it is located at the unconformity contact separating late Paleoproterozoic to Mesoproterozoic sandstone of the Athabasca Group from middle Paleoproterozoic metasedimentary gneiss and
plutonic rocks of the Wollaston Group.
The Athabasca Group appears largely undeformed with a maximum preserved thickness of about 1,500 metres
within the eastern part of the Athabasca basin. The Manitou Falls (MF) formation sequence of sandstone is interpreted to have been deposited in an intra-continental sedimentary basin that was filled by fluviatile terrestrial quartz sandstone and
conglomerate as units of the MF Formation. On the eastern side of the basin, the sandstone units of the MF Formation, and the Wollaston Group metasedimentary gneiss that unconformably lie immediately beneath them, host most of the uranium
mineralization. The sandstone which overlies the basement rock is saturated and water conductive, resulting in significant water pressures at depth.
The Lower Pelitic unit of the Wollaston Group, which lies directly on the Archean granitoid basement, is considered to be the most favourable unit for
uranium mineralization. During the Hudsonian orogeny (1.8 – 1.9 million years), the group underwent polyphase deformation and upper amphibolite facies metamorphism, with local greenschist facies retrograde metamorphism. The Hudsonian
orogeny was followed by a long period of erosion and weathering along with the development of a paleoweathering profile that is locally preserved at, and beneath, the unconformity.

7.3
Property geology
The lithostructural setting of the McArthur River deposit consists of three principal geological units, namely the overlying Athabasca sandstone, the
underlying metasedimentary gneisses of the Wollaston Domain, and the P2 fault zone.
The Athabasca Group is represented by sandstones (with lesser
conglomerates and siltstones) of the MFd, MFc, MFb and MFa subunits of the Manitou Falls (MF) Formation. The preserved thickness of the MF Formation near the mine is 480 to 560 metres forming a major aquifer in the area despite the strong local
silicification.
The Wollaston Domain stratigraphy in the property area has been locally divided into three blocks, based on their lithological and
structural characteristics:
•
A hanging wall block consisting of biotite and garnet gneiss, and calc-silicate.
•
A middle block consisting of cordierite gneiss, graphitic cordierite gneiss, biotite gneiss, and arkose with the main
graphitic fault zone situated within the upper 20 metre section.

A quartzite block underlying the two upper units and consisting of massive to faintly laminated quartzite. This quartzite

The northeast trending P2 reverse fault is the dominant structural feature of the McArthur River deposit (see

Host rock alteration has played a critical role in
the development of rock strength and geochemistry of the McArthur River deposit. Unlike other significant uranium deposits in the Athabasca Basin, the McArthur River deposit is not surrounded by a vertically and laterally extensive alteration halo.
Strong to intense hydrothermal alteration is dominantly locally distributed along major faults associated with mineralization and brecciation zones along the P2 structure. Significant sandstone, clay and hematite alteration is encountered within the
upper extensions of the P2 above Zone 2 and Zone 4; likely a result of increased fracture density resulting in higher permeability and poor ground conditions.
Outside the P2 structural zone, pervasive silicification is the dominant alteration within the sandstone which hindered the development of extensive
alteration halos. Chloritization, dravitization and clay alteration are the main alteration types observed within the basement hanging wall block.
7.4
Mineralization
Uranium mineralization has been delineated from surface drilling over a strike length of 2,700 metres and occurs at depths ranging between 500 metres to
640 metres below surface. The relative position and width of mineralization is variable along strike; however, the most consistent, high-grade mineralization occurs proximal to the main graphitic reverse fault by the upthrust basement block. Less
consistent and generally lower grade mineralization occurs downdip along this fault contact between basement rock and sandstone. The main part of the mineralization, generally at the upper part of the basement wedge, averages 12.7 metres in width
and has a vertical extent ranging between 50 and 120 metres.
The deposit consists of nine distinct mineralized areas and three under-explored
surface defined mineralized showings. The mineralized areas with reported mineral resources are named Zone 1, Zone 2, Zone 3, Zone 4, Zone 4 South, Zone A, Zone B, McA North 1 and McA North 2. The mineralized showings are designated as McA North 3,
McA North 4 and McA South 1.

competent sandstone.

Deposit types
The McArthur River deposit is an unconformity-related uranium deposit. Deposits of this type are believed to have formed through an oxidation-reduction
reaction at or near an unconformity where oxygenated fluids meet with reducing fluids and/or lithologies along fault zones. At the McArthur River deposit, the unconformity surface is a contact between Athabasca Group sandstones and underlying
metasedimentary rocks of the Wollaston Domain while graphitic faults, such as the P2, provided the conduit for interaction of oxidizing sandstone fluids with reducing basement fluids.
This geological setting is analogous to the uranium deposits at Key Lake, McClean Lake, Collins Bay and Cigar Lake, which the McArthur River deposit
shares many similarities with, including general structural setting, mineralogy, geochemistry, host rock association and the age of the mineralization. While the degree of alteration may vary, types of alteration found in these deposits are
generally similar and include chloritization, hematization, kaolinization, dravitization, illitization, pyritization, desilicification and silicification. Additional information on uranium deposit types can be found on the International Atomic
Energy Agency website (IAEA, 2020).
The main difference between the McArthur River deposit and the other deposits is its very high grade and
tonnage, overall size, the absence of an extensive hydrothermal clay alteration zone in the sandstone above the uranium mineralization and its occurrence within a reverse fault wedge.
The McArthur River deposit is similar to other Athabasca Basin uranium deposits in that the sandstone that overlies the basement rock is saturated, water
conductive and has significant water pressure at depth.

13.1
Metallurgical testing for McArthur River ore processing facilities
The McArthur River ore processing flowsheet was largely based on the use of conventional mineral processing equipment, with targeted testwork undertaken
to confirm design assumptions and adapt technology for the mine’s unique high-grade ore. Because external laboratories could not accept radioactive feed, simulated ore materials were used extensively in
off-site
testing. Historical metallurgical and operational testwork completed prior to mine
start-up
in 2000 included the following major program areas:
•
Slurry transport and handling systems consisting of pipeline flow tests conducted at the SRCGL facility to establish slurry
transport characteristics and pumping requirements.
•
Full-scale slurry container prototype testing, including gravity and vacuum unloading trials and cold weather drop tests,
to verify safe handling and haulage. Truck and trailer transportation trials were also completed to confirm road transport performance.
•
Uranium ore scanning and monitoring consisting of evaluating radiation scanning systems for loaded slurry containers to
support ore monitoring and transportation requirements. Although automated scanning systems were tested, manual and CCTV-based inspection methods were ultimately adopted for operations.
•
Ore reclaiming and crushing alternatives including testing of
water-jet
slurry
reclaim technology for recovering settled ore from storage containers, together with evaluations of a
low-profile

Ore handling underground consisting of testing a prototype transportable mining unit for recovery and pumping of reamed

•
Process instrumentation which included evaluation of instrumentation capable of simultaneously measuring slurry density and
uranium grade.
•
Laboratory-scale Bond Work Index grinding tests were completed to support SAG mill sizing, together with slurry settling
and thickening tests at the target grind size to support thickener design.
This comprehensive testwork program verified that the
selected process flowsheet and equipment were suitable for processing McArthur River ore. In addition, bench-scale and pilot-scale testing completed at the Key Lake site confirmed that the existing Key Lake mill circuit could process McArthur River
slurry with only minor circuit modifications to accommodate the different ore feed characteristics.
Since commissioning in 2000, the integrated
McArthur River ore processing system, including slurry hoisting, transportation, and water treatment circuits, has operated reliably, with ongoing optimization initiatives implemented to improve efficiency and maintain performance. A notable
improvement was implemented in late 2009 when the grinding circuit classification system was converted from static screens to hydrocyclones. The hydrocyclone circuit classified material based on specific gravity as well as particle size, resulting
in improved downstream uranium recovery through preferential grinding of denser uranium-bearing particles. The change also significantly reduced operational issues, including slurry segregation, pipeline plugging, and sanding within process tanks at
both McArthur River and Key Lake.
Initial McArthur River water treatment testwork and operating programs have supported chemical precipitation,

13.2
Metallurgical testing for Key Lake milling facilities
The original Key Lake uranium mill has been in operation since 1983 and was adapted in 2000 to process McArthur River ore. Prior to commissioning,
bench-scale metallurgical testwork conducted at the Key Lake site laboratory confirmed that McArthur River ore could be processed successfully through the existing Key Lake circuit with only minor flowsheet modifications. Following startup, mill
throughput was increased beyond the original design capacity through a series of operational optimizations, including adjustments to solvent extraction (SX) chemistry that enabled higher uranium loadings in both the extraction and stripping
circuits.
Beginning in 2006, testwork was undertaken to evaluate and optimize molybdenum and selenium removal from Key Lake mill effluent. The
resulting process modifications and commissioning of the Mo/Se treatment circuit in 2009 significantly reduced molybdenum and selenium concentrations and loadings in treated effluent, supporting long-term compliance with environmental performance
objectives. See
Section
20.3
for further information.
Between 2006 and 2008, Cameco undertook a mill revitalization study
focused on the SX circuit. The study included pilot-scale evaluations of pulsed-column SX contactors as a potential replacement for mixer-settlers, as well as strong-acid stripping trials as an alternative to the conventional ammonia stripping
process. Neither alternative flowsheet was ultimately adopted. Instead, selective modification and debottlenecking of the existing mixer-settler circuit increased SX capacity and efficiency, while the strong-acid stripping option did not demonstrate
sufficient benefit relative to the established ammonia strip process.
Metallurgical testwork completed in 2016 addressed the impact of cementitious
solids introduced from McArthur River backfill on the Key Lake milling circuits. The testwork demonstrated that addition of a coagulant to the primary thickener feed mitigates silica gel formation, reduces the effects of cement dilution, and
eliminates the need to operate the legacy gravity concentrator stage. Implementation of this modification improved SX performance and overall circuit reliability while also improving processing capability of the cementitious solids and recovery of
the contained values of uranium.
Overall, the Key Lake mill flowsheet including grinding, leaching, counter-current decantation (CCD), SX,
precipitation, calcining, and associated support circuit have historically achieved high uranium recoveries and demonstrated robust performance through ongoing incremental optimization. No significant flowsheet changes have been required and no new
deleterious elements or processing factors have been identified in McArthur River ore that would require additional metallurgical treatment beyond the established Key Lake flowsheet.

13.4
Metallurgical assumptions and risks
The production schedule assumes that future ore feed remains comparable to ore previously processed through McArthur River–Key Lake processes with
respect to mineralogy, uranium recovery and liberation characteristics, grindability, leachability, reagent demand, slurry rheology, settling behaviour, solid-liquid separation performance, and constituents that could affect product quality, long
term tailings stability, water treatment, or environmental performance.
While ongoing data collection indicates that the McArthur River orebodies
are generally similar in nature, material changes in these characteristics, including increased concentrations of clay or silica fines, acid-consuming gangue minerals, sulphides, soluble salts, or deleterious metals, metalloids, or radionuclides,
could adversely affect throughput, uranium recovery, reagent consumption, tailings characteristics, water treatment and environmental performance, operating costs, or the potential economic extraction of the ore.
Accordingly, mineralogical, metallurgical, environmental, and geochemical characterization should be completed for future mining areas where geological
information indicates significant differences from previously mined and processed ore to validate the assumptions used in the production schedule.
The mill throughput test demonstrated short-duration operation of the leaching, CCD and SX circuits at uranium throughput equivalent to an annualized
production rate of approximately 25 million lb U
3
O
8
per year. However, the test was limited to approximately seven days and did not
demonstrate sustained annual performance at this production rate.
Sustained operation at higher production rates will depend on successful
commissioning of the planned mill upgrades and continued reliable performance of the processing circuits and associated equipment. Accordingly, some residual metallurgical and operational risk remains associated with sustained operation at rates
approaching the licensed production capacity.

Factors that could materially affect the mineral reserves estimate
Like other commodities, the uranium industry is cyclical. Since McArthur River began production, spot prices have ranged from less than $10 to more than
$130 US per pound. It is possible that the timing of these cycles could have a material adverse impact on Cameco’s future production decisions and declared reserves, however, the McArthur River mineral reserves are not significantly sensitive

There are no relevant factors known to

Water inflows
A significant risk to
development and production is from a water inflow event. Despite the mitigation measures Cameco has put in place; there remains a possibility of a water inflow during mine development and production mining. The consequences of a water inflow at
McArthur River will depend on its magnitude, location and timing of any such event, but could include a significant interruption or reduction in production, a material increase in costs, a loss of mineral reserves or require Cameco to give notice to
many of its customers that it is declaring an interruption in planned uranium supply. Such consequences could have a material adverse impact on Cameco. Water inflows are generally not insurable.
Geotechnical challenges
Mine development and
production are occurring in faulted and altered rock which present risks and challenges in executing the mine plan. In addition, ground stress induced by artificial ground freezing and proximal development has resulted in unplanned rehabilitation
and/or localized production interruptions. Lastly, as the mine ages, increased rehab is expected to be required.
To date, mining extraction, rehab
and recovery plans have been successful, however there remains a risk of stope caving or extensive drift abandonment that could potentially result in production deferral or a partial loss of mineral reserves.
In addition, development through the Zone 4 clay is planned to use mass ground freezing. Although mass freezing has been previously used for production
mining, this will be the first time that the site has used mass freezing specifically for mine development. The site believes the mine design and schedules are sufficiently conservative to address reasonably unforeseen conditions; however, there
remains a risk that schedules cannot be executed as planned, or that mine designs cannot be fully implemented. This could potentially result in production deferral and potentially a partial loss of mineral reserves in the Zone 4 clay area.

Mining methods
This section describes the technical aspects of the underground mine, including hydrogeology, radiological and geological conditions and controls, mine
development, ground freezing, mine equipment, underground infrastructure, mining methods, backfill requirements and forecast production rates.
16.1
Mine design parameters
The McArthur River deposit presents unique challenges that are not typical of traditional hard or soft rock mines. These challenges are the result of
mining in or near high pressure ground water in challenging ground conditions with significant radiation hazards due to the high-grade uranium mineralization.
Mining methods are selected on their ability to mitigate risks associated with high pressure water, radiation hazards and challenging ground conditions.
Mine designs are coordinated through the site’s mine engineering department. When required, third party technical experts are included in the design process. In general, designs are conservative to protect the high value deposit and to avoid a
serious to catastrophic water inflow event.
All new development areas, new production areas and new mining methods undergo internal technical review
and approval. These reviews are carried out to determine the level of risk associated with the planned work and if the controls identified are sufficient to mitigate that risk.
Standard controls utilized at McArthur River for managing hydrological, radiological and geotechnical risks are described below.
Hydrological conditions and controls
All the
mineralized areas discovered to date at McArthur River are in or partially in water-bearing ground with pressures ranging from 680 to 850 psi. Hydrological conditions can be divided into two regions:
Water-bearing:
The water-bearing region consists of all the rock units above the unconformity contact (conglomerate and sandstone). Drawdown
testing has demonstrated that the fracture patterns, along with water-bearing joints and bedding planes, are directly connected to the surface groundwater table. The sandstone and conglomerate itself, however, is not porous.
Water flow rates through the fractures and joints will vary, but typically the highest conductive pathways are associated with the P2 fault as the
brittle, flat lying sandstone has been well fractured by the tectonic forces of the reverse fault.
Basement:
The basement consists of all the
rock units below the unconformity contact (biotite gneiss, cordierite gneiss, quartzite plus some minor units). The basement units are typically dry but can contain open water pathways to the sandstone. These pathways typically consist of faults,
fractures, joints and unsecured drillholes. The risk of intersecting water pathways increases with proximity to the unconformity contact.
To reduce
the inherent risk of an inflow, the high-pressure water sources are isolated from active development and production areas. To date, McArthur River has relied on pressure grouting and ground freezing to successfully mitigate the risks of the
high-pressure groundwater (see
Figure
16-1
).

Radiological conditions and controls
Gamma radiation, radon gas, radon progeny and long-lived radioactive dust (LLRD) hazards are all present at McArthur River. The degree of radiological
risk and controls required is a function of location. The McArthur River deposit can be divided into three radiological regions:
Massive
Mineralization:
The region consists of known ore zones and areas of continuous mineralization. The ore regions are well identified ahead of development and typically consist of pitchblende and coffinite. Grades are typically greater than 10% U
3
O
8
but can be greater than 80%
U
3
O
8
in localized areas.
Transition Mineralization:
The transition areas consist of the ground that surrounds the orebody and typically contains low levels of
mineralization due to small ore stringers that offshoot from the main orebody. Due to the random nature of these ore stringers, they are often difficult to model and identify. Transitions can be sharp when moving east-west (on the mine grid), but
highly variable when moving sub vertically along the P2 fault trend.
Non-Mineralized:
The region
consists of all the rock units located a sufficient distance away from the mineralized areas where the conditions for deposition were not present during the formation of the ore body.
Gamma, radon, radon progeny and LLRD hazards are all present in the mineralized areas. Gamma and LLRD hazards are not present in the
non-mineralized
areas. Typically, water intersected in
non-mineralized
areas does not contain significant amounts of radon unless proximal to the mineralization.
Controls must be in place to ensure that the regulatory exposure limits are not exceeded. Typical design controls used at McArthur River include avoiding
development in high-grade mineralization whenever possible and practical, shielding of gamma sources, direct exhaust ventilation, point source ventilation capture of radon gas, remote technology whenever possible and practical, ground freezing and
pressure grouting to prevent entry of radon-bearing water into the mine workings.
Geotechnical conditions and controls

Development design
Development at McArthur River is classified into three categories: low, medium, and high-risk development based on hydrological, geotechnical and
radiological risks.
Low and medium risk development accounts for most of the development while high risk development accounts for less than 5% of
the overall development. High risk development is typically associated with development through highly fractured and altered areas associated with the P2 fault or near the unconformity contact without freeze protection.
Probe and grout covers are maintained ahead of the development face to gather geological, geotechnical, hydrological and radiological information and to
pressure grout off water-bearing structures. Formal probe and grout reviews are jointly carried out by the site’s mine engineering and geology departments prior to advancing development.
Drift dimensions vary depending on the location and end use. Normal travel ways are excavated as a 5 m x 5 m drift with an arched back. Standard minimum
ground support consists of 2.4 m #7 rebar bolts on a 1.2 m x 1.2 m pattern and
6-gauge
welded wire mesh screen.
Standard ground support is generally sufficient for all development classed as low risk. Ground support for medium and high-risk development plus
intersections and large spans are assessed individually and the appropriate level of support installed.
Old mine workings no longer required are
typically used for
non-combustible
contaminated waste disposal, or disposal of uneconomic waste rock. Abandoned mine workings that may pose a long-term stability concern and water inflow risk are tight filled.
Completed and planned development is maintained in three-dimensional mine design software, which is integrated with the geological model and
drillhole database.

Ground freezing
Ground freezing is used at McArthur River to prevent or restrict high pressure ground water from entering the mine. In addition, it is also used for
ground strengthening in areas with very low rock strengths. Typical applications of ground freezing at McArthur are the following:
Freeze
wall
A freeze wall (or shell) is a region of frozen ground that completely isolates a region of the underground from water-bearing ground.
Ground within the freeze wall is distinguished by the lack of water
re-charge
and by static water pressure significantly below the water-bearing ground outside the freeze wall. To achieve this, the freeze
shell is made of interlocking freeze walls and is anchored into
non-water-bearing
basement rock. Once a freeze wall is established, the area is depressurized and drained. Development and ore extraction can
proceed within the inside of the freeze walls, with a significantly lowered water inflow risk.
Established freeze walls currently exist for Zone 1,
Zone 2 and Zone 4. The Zone 4 South freeze wall cap has been activated, with the north, south and west walls remaining to be completed (
Figure
16-6
). Freeze walls are planned for all the remaining
undeveloped mine zones containing mineral reserves (Zone 3, Zone A and Zone B).
Figure
16-6:
Current
Freeze Wall Status as of September 30, 2026
Mass freezing
Mass freezing is a
method of bulk freezing a large area for the purposes of both water control and ground stabilization. It is typically considered suitable for areas where the rock mass is exceptionally weak and at risk of uncontrolled
cave-ins
without strengthening by ground freezing. To successfully mass freeze an area, parallel freeze rings must be drilled close enough to allow the ground freezing to connect between rings. Mine
development or extraction would take place between freeze rings.
Mass freezing was utilized as part of the boxhole test program and will be utilized
in the Zone 4 clay area for both mine development and production. Mass freezing has been successfully applied at Cameco’s Cigar Lake mine.
Freeze shield
A freeze shield is a
region of frozen ground that does not completely isolate a region from water-bearing ground. This type of freeze structure is typically used where simply impeding the flow of water through a water-bearing structure is sufficient.
A freeze shield was used to successfully establish to the
530-7300E
Zone 4 freeze drift where water conductive
sub-faulting
was known to pass closely to the planned excavation.

Mining methods
At McArthur River, there are three approved mining methods: raisebore mining, blasthole stope mining, and boxhole mining, which have been used for mining
approximately 386 million pounds U
3
O
8
since production
start-up
in 1999.
Raisebore mining
Raisebore mining is
suitable for massive high-grade zones where there is access both above and below the ore zone. The raise opening created by mechanical cutting has proven to be very stable making this method favourable for mining the weaker rock mass areas of the
deposit. In addition, holes can be drilled accurately over long distances when compared to traditional production drilling, eliminating the need for sublevel access.
A raisebore chamber is developed in waste above the ore zone and an extraction chamber is developed in waste below the ore zone. A raisebore drill is set
up in the raisebore chamber and a standpipe is installed. A pilot hole is then drilled to breakthrough into the lower extraction chamber. All cuttings from pilot drilling are contained and piped away to avoid radiation contamination of the work
area.
Once breakthrough occurs, the reamer is installed and mechanical cutting begins (reaming). Reaming continues through waste and into the ore.
Raisebore cuttings are mucked remotely as required. All cuttings from production raises are scanned for ore grade estimates and delivered to the appropriate dump locations.
Reaming stops at the upper ore contact below the raisebore chamber. The reamer is lowered to the brow of the open hole and final muck cleanup of the
chamber is carried out. The reamer is then lowered to the sill and the backfill gantry is installed for head cover protection. The chamber and reamer are washed down and then the reamer and rods are removed. Once all the rods are out, the raisebore
is moved to the next scheduled production raise.
Once the rods are removed, backfilling can begin. This is done in three stages (plug, second and
final) using concrete as backfill material. The bottom of the raise is sealed with a backfill gantry or remote plug tool and the initial plug pour is placed from the bottom of the raise using a portable concrete pump. After the first pour has set,
the second pour is placed from the raisebore chamber through the pilot collar.
After the second pour is set, the plug is bolted for ground stability
purposes. The final pour is then completed from the raisebore chamber through the pilot hole.
Production raises are designed to overlap each other
to maximize recovery of the high-grade ore. Mine recoveries are typically 97.5% with a small amount of ore lost between the cusps between the circular raises. As the production raises normally have smooth walls, CMS surveys are not typically done
unless wall sloughing has occurred.

Blasthole stope mining
After successfully completing six test stopes in ore, blasthole stoping was approved by the CNSC as an extraction method in November 2013. Since
approval, the use of this method has expanded to the point where approximately 70% of the ore is now extracted using this method.
Blasthole stoping
is planned in areas where blastholes can be accurately drilled and small stable stopes excavated without jeopardizing freeze wall integrity. Blasthole stope mining has shown an advantage over raisebore mining on overall extraction efficiency by
reducing underground development, concrete consumption, mineralized waste generation, and improving extraction cycle time. Use of this method has significantly reduced McArthur River’s overall operating costs.
Drill access is developed in waste above the ore and undercut mucking access is developed in waste below the ore. A raisebore slot is excavated and
drillholes placed around the slot. Drill standpipes are used to contain drill cuttings to avoid radiation contamination of the work area. The drillholes are then blasted into the slot, typically as several small blasts. Blasting slightly into the
waste is often done to ensure recovery of all the high-grade ore. A waste cap is typically left at the top of the stope.
Blasted ore is remotely
mucked from the raise draw point and scanned for grade estimates and delivered to the appropriate location. Once blasting is complete, the stope is backfilled in the same manner as a production raisebore hole described above. A small number of
stopes with no planned adjacent mining are filled with dry waste muck.
All drill and blast stopes are 3D scanned to monitor for ore recoveries and
reconcile production. Additional blast holes, recovery stopes or deferral to adjacent stope mining can be used to recover missed ore. On an individual excavation stope basis, recoveries can be highly variable, however, final ore recoveries are
assumed to be 99%.

Recovery methods
17.1
Overview
The McArthur River mine and Key Lake mill operate as an integrated mine–mill complex licensed to produce up to 25 million lb U
3
O
8
annually. Ore is prepared onsite at McArthur River as two principal feed streams: high-grade uranium slurry and
low-grade
mineralized material. Both streams are transported to Key Lake, where they are blended and processed to produce calcined uranium ore concentrate.
The Key Lake mill, originally constructed to process ore from the Key Lake deposits, was modified to process high-grade McArthur River feed, including
the addition of dedicated slurry receiving, storage and handling facilities.
At McArthur River, mined material is characterized underground
primarily by radiometric scanning and directed to the appropriate handling stream based on grade and inventory requirements. Material above 3.5% U
3
O
8
is processed through the underground grinding and thickening circuits to produce a high-grade slurry for transport to Key Lake. The 3.5% U
3
O
8
threshold corresponds to the 3.0%
uranium-by-mass
limit for Low Specific Activity Material
(LSA-I)
under the Packaging and Transport of Nuclear Substances Regulations, 2015 (PTNSR); consequently, material above this grade is transported as slurry rather than as dry ore. The slurry is pumped to surface,
stored in
air-agitated
pachucas, blended as required, and loaded into dedicated containers for transport to Key Lake.
Material below 3.5% U
3
O
8
is hoisted to
surface and transported to the Key Lake
low-grade
blend pads for processing. Uneconomic mineralized material is retained underground for dry backfill or tight filling when space is available and practical to
do so, otherwise it becomes part of the
low-grade
mill blend.
Upon arrival at Key Lake, high-grade slurry is
received at the ore slurry receiving facility and stored in
air-agitated
pachuca tanks.
Low-grade
mineralization from McArthur River, together with legacy mineralized
waste blend material from the original Key Lake Deilmann and Gaertner mining operations stored at Key Lake, is reclaimed from the blend pads, ground with water, slurried, and thickened. The resulting
low-grade
slurry is blended with the McArthur River high-grade slurry to produce a controlled mill feed, typically between 4 and 6% U
3
O
8
. The
planned average leach feed grade over the LOM is 4.99% U
3
O
8
.
Uranium recovery is then completed through the Key Lake milling circuit, which includes acid leaching, counter-current decantation (CCD) and
clarification, solvent extraction (SX) purification, ammonium diuranate precipitation, dewatering, calcination to U
3
O
8
and packaging of
the final uranium ore concentrate product. Process tailings and neutralized residues are managed at Key Lake, with tailings deposited in the Deilmann
in-pit
tailings management facility.
In 2025, Cameco initiated a multi-year capital investment program comprising targeted infrastructure and process upgrades intended to support reliable
operation at planned production rates while providing production optionality and flexibility up to the Key Lake mill’s licensed annual production capacity of 25 million pounds U
3
O
8
. The program includes replacement and expansion of selected process circuits, renewal of aging infrastructure and upgrades to key utility and support systems to increase processing capacity,
improve reliability and redundancy, address equipment obsolescence, and support long-term operation. Planned upgrades encompass several major process and support areas, including leaching, CCD, crystallization, calcination, yellowcake handling,
utilities, electrical systems, process control infrastructure and other site services. These initiatives build upon earlier investments in process debottlenecking, replacement of major utility systems, electrical infrastructure upgrades, and
enhancements to effluent treatment, including molybdenum and selenium removal circuits.
Collectively, these modernization initiatives are intended
to maintain Key Lake as a reliable processing facility for McArthur River mineral reserves and provide additional capacity and operational redundancy.

17.2
McArthur River handling and processing of mineralized material
Mined mineralized material is characterized using radiometric scanning and transported using load-haul-dump vehicles. The material is initially separated
based on grade and stockpiled in designated underground coarse ore storages. When possible and practical, uneconomic mineralized material is kept underground and disposed of in either old mine workings or as a dry backfill. All remaining material
not disposed of underground is then either directed to the underground grinding circuit or hoisted to surface.
Low-grade
mineralized material is transferred by load-haul-dump vehicles to a loading pocket at the base of Shaft
1. Oversize material is reduced using a rock breaker mounted above a storage bin until it passes through the grizzly screen. The grizzly undersize is hoisted to surface and hauled to a designated
low-grade
mineralization stockpile pad. This material is then later transported to Key Lake via truck and trailers and added to the Key Lake
low-grade
mineralization blend pads for incorporation into mill feed.
The high-grade mineralization is transferred by load-haul-dump vehicles to the semi-autogenous grinding (SAG) grizzly covered hopper. A rock breaker
mounted over the hopper is used to break the oversize material until it passes through the grizzly screen. Grizzly undersize material is then fed by belt conveyor to the SAG mill.
The grinding circuit consists of a 4.6 metre long by 2.9 metre diameter SAG mill in closed circuit with hydrocyclones and a safety screen. The
mill discharges through discharge grates onto a blind trommel which removes tramp metal via a magnet. The trommel discharge is pumped to the hydrocyclones and the hydrocyclone underflow is returned to the SAG mill for further grinding.
Hydrocyclone overflow is pumped to the safety screen and the screen undersize is thickened in a
13-metre
diameter
thickener. The thickener underflow is pumped to and stored in the underground ore slurry storage

TECHNICAL REPORT 102
tank before being pumped to surface. Safety screen oversize is returned to the SAG mill for further grinding while the thickener overflow is stored in a dam before being pumped to the surface ore
loadout thickener.
Contaminated water from collection points throughout the mine as well as drill cuttings are pumped to two underground
overflow-type surge tanks where the settled solids are intermittently
re-slurried
and transferred to the SAG mill by tank bottom-mounted solids recovery systems. The tank overflow is pumped to a second
13-metre
diameter thickener for clarification, and the thickener overflow is stored in a second dam before being pumped to surface for treatment. The thickener underflow is mixed with the ore slurry and pumped to
surface.
Following pumping to surface, the high-grade slurry is stored in four
air-agitated
pachuca storage
tanks. Slurry discharged from the pachucas is blended in an ore mix tank to a maximum grade of 25% U
3
O
8
. After excess water is removed
from the blended ore slurry in the
15-metre

3

Depending on mine grade and uranium production requirements, average annual ore trucking for both high-grade slurry and
low-grade

17.3
Key Lake activities
High-grade ore slurry arriving at the Key Lake ore slurry receiving plant is unloaded from the truck-mounted containers by a vacuum aspiration system and
pumped to one of four large
air-agitated
slurry storage pachucas.
McArthur River
low-grade
mineralization and legacy
low-grade
mineralized waste stored at Key Lake on the
low-grade
mineralization blend pads is
delivered to the grinding circuit grizzly by loader. The grinding circuit consists of a SAG mill in open circuit with a sizing screen. The screen oversize reports to the ball mill in closed circuit with two sizing screens. The undersize from all
three screens report to the neutral thickener. The neutral thickener overflow is combined with industrial water to be
re-used
throughout the circuit.
High-grade ore slurry is withdrawn from the ore storage pachucas and pumped to the blending tank where it is mixed with the neutral thickener underflow.
The resulting slurry is pumped to one of three storage pachucas located in the leaching plant. Blending is necessary as the original Key Lake processing facilities were not designed from a radiation protection perspective to accommodate the high ore
grades found at McArthur River. In addition to reducing the radiation exposure in the mill, the dilution of the high-grade ore serves two other purposes: recovery of uranium from the
low-grade
mineralized
material, and final disposal of the
low-grade
mineralized waste.
The uranium is leached from the ore in the
atmospheric leach pachuca and three continuous stirred tank reactors, while uranium-bearing solution is separated from waste solids in the CCD wash circuit. Sulphuric acid, steam and oxygen are used in the leach vessels to dissolve the uranium from
the ore.
The CCD circuit consists of eight thickeners in series and a Primary Thickener which acts as a final polishing clarifier prior to SX. The
slurry flow is counter current to the wash water. The slurry moves from thickener one to thickener eight, while the wash water moves from thickener eight to one. The uranium-rich CCD overflow is pumped to the clarifier while the CCD underflow, with
minimal residual uranium, is treated and sent to the Deilmann tailings management facility for long-term disposal.
In the solvent extraction plant,
the clarified overflow pregnant solution is concentrated and purified by mixing with an organic solvent. The uranium transfers from the aqueous solution to the organic phase leaving behind most of the dissolved impurities. The organic solvent,
loaded with uranium, is contacted with ammonium sulphate solution causing the uranium to transfer back to the aqueous phase known as loaded strip solution. A molybdenum removal circuit treats the loaded strip solution to remove molybdenum, an
undesirable impurity in the final product.

Using ammonia, uranium is precipitated from the loaded strip solution in the precipitation tank as
ammonium diuranate. The precipitate is dewatered in the yellowcake thickener followed by a centrifuge then calcined to U
3
O
8
in a furnace.
The final calcined uranium ore concentrate is packed in 210 litre drums for shipment to refineries around the world.
Excess ammonium sulphate is
recovered from the yellowcake thickener overflow by evaporating the water and drying the resulting product, which is sold locally for use as a high purity fertilizer.
Contaminated water from the dewatering system associated with the depleted Gaertner and Deilmann open pits at Key Lake is treated in a reverse osmosis
(RO) plant with the permeate used as industrial water within the mill circuits and Key Lake fire systems as required.
Reject water from the reverse
osmosis plant along with waste solvent extraction solution (raffinate) is sent to the bulk neutralization (BN) plant where the streams are neutralised with lime and other reagents to precipitate dissolved impurities. The resulting solids are
combined with the treated CCD underflow and pumped to the Deilmann tailings management facility for long-term disposal. The treated water is sampled and released to one of four batch release monitoring ponds. If all federal and provincial
regulations are met, the treated water is released to the environment. If not, the pond is recycled through the bulk neutralization plant until the treated effluent becomes suitable for release.
The powerhouse/utilities/acid plant/oxygen plant complex provides acid, steam and oxygen for leaching and backup power as required.
A site-wide modernization and efficiency program was implemented at Key Lake from 2020 to 2022 during the care and maintenance period. The program was
intended to reduce future costs, improve safety, and increase operational flexibility ahead of restart through a coordinated set of automation, digitalization and process optimization initiatives across the Key Lake mill and the McArthur River
interface. Key outcomes included expanded process automation, improved data integration, and implementation of an automated calcine packaging system, which significantly reduced manual handling and operator exposure. Collectively, these upgrades
helped reduce the risks associated with the 2022 restart and supported a stable
ramp-up
to production in late 2022.

Water treatment
Water that has been in contact with high-grade ore is collected and directed to the Primary Water Treatment Plant (PWTP). The PWTP has a treatment
capacity of 80 m³/h and is designed to treat water containing elevated concentrations of selenium, molybdenum, arsenic, and other constituents associated with ore handling and processing activities. Treatment in the primary circuit consists of
chemical conditioning and precipitation, followed by clarification in a lamella thickener. Solids generated in the primary circuit are directed to a filter press for dewatering, while clarified overflow is routed to the Secondary Water Treatment
Plant (SWTP) for further treatment.
Excess shaft water, surface and underground water that did not have high contact with high-grade ore is directed
to surface collection ponds, which provide surge storage and operational flexibility for site water management. Water treatment is provided by the SWTP, which has a treatment capacity of 750 m³/h. The SWTP receives clarified overflow from the
PWTP together with water from the surface collection ponds. The secondary treatment circuit primarily removes uranium, radium, dissolved base metals, and other constituents requiring treatment prior to discharge or reuse through chemical
precipitation, flocculation, clarification, filtration, and pH adjustment. Treated water from the SWTP is routed to treated water storage and monitoring ponds prior to release.
A portion of the treated water is recycled on site for industrial water use. Remaining treated water is released. Prior to discharge, treated water is
sampled and evaluated against applicable federal and provincial discharge criteria. Water that does not meet discharge requirements is recycled through the treatment system until compliance is achieved.
Solids generated during water treatment are dewatered using a filter press and transferred to the
low-grade
mineralization storage pads, where it is blended with

mineralization and transported to the Key Lake as part of the ore stream.

Water treatment
Key Lake operates two principal impacted-water treatment systems: the RO plant and the BN circuit within the mill. The RO plant treats water recovered
from the Deilmann and Gaertner dewatering systems and associated groundwater-recovery infrastructure. Treated permeate is reused for industrial or fire-water service where required or discharged to Horsefly Lake in the McDonald Creek drainage. RO
concentrate is directed to the BN circuit for further treatment.

ponds, underdrains and freshwater-diversion infrastructure. These systems provide containment, storage and operational flexibility rather than constituting separate metallurgical process stages.

an above ground tailings management facility (AGTMF) constructed in 1983, where tailings are stored within compacted till
embankments. Cameco has not deposited tailings here since 1996 and continues to utilize the facility for disposal of radiologically contaminated industrial materials.
•
the Deilmann tailings management facility (DTMF) was commissioned in 1996 following completion of mining in the eastern
portion of the Deilmann pit in 1995.
The DTMF was initially operated in a subaerial tailings deposition mode. A sand envelop in
the DTMF was designed to allow excess water to drain to a drainage blanket underlying the tailings at the bottom of the pit and then to dewatering pumps in a raise well connected by a drift to the drainage blanket. The DTMF was converted from
subaerial tailings disposal system to a subaqueous tailings disposal system in 1998 following approval by the CNSC and SMOE, allowing the water level in the pit to rise above the tailings surface. The

remaining Key Lake tailings were deposited subaqueously followed by the deposition of tailings from McArthur River ore in 2000 to present. Flooding of the pit was accomplished by reducing the
pumping rate from peripheral dewatering wells that had been used to dewater the pit during mining and construction.
Beginning in July 2001, when
rising water contacted the lowermost sections of the outwash sand in the Key Lake trough, periodic sloughing of the pit walls in the western portion of the DTMF was experienced. As a result of these events, Cameco undertook a number of actions to
minimize potential damage from future events, including infrastructure relocation along vulnerable sections of the pit crest, installation of slope monitoring stations, and creation of a
30-metre
wide
“restricted” zone along the perimeter of the pit crest at the west end of the DTMF. Cameco also performed several studies to better understand the pit wall sloughing mechanism and initiated mitigation measures for prevention of
sloughing.
Studies showed that stabilizing and reducing water levels in the pit enhanced the stability of the pit walls, which reduced the risk of
pit wall sloughing. Cameco doubled its dewatering treatment capacity, allowing it to maintain and prevent further increases to the water level in the pit. Controlling the water level was an effective interim measure in managing further sloughing
while work to cut back the slopes for long-term stabilization was completed. In 2009, regulators approved Cameco’s plan for the long-term stabilization of the DTMF pit walls. Cameco implemented the plan in phases based on DTMF water level and
risk. Work was completed in 2013 on the west wall and 2016 on the south side. The final phase on the northeast slope was completed in 2019. Controlled
re-flooding
of the DTMF resumed in 2016 and continued
through 2024. Flooding was halted at water elevation 506.0 m in May 2025. To ensure hydraulic containment of the Deilmann North Waste Rock Pile (DNWRP) is maintained, the water level of the DTMF was held steady at 506.0 for the remainder of 2025.
The water management strategy will be to continue holding the DTMF water level at 506.0 m while monitoring and assessing the hydraulic containment. Adjustments to this strategy will be dependent on the results of the surveillance program.
In 2014, the CNSC and the SMOE approved an increase in Key Lake’s tailings capacity. Cameco now expects to have sufficient tailings capacity to
mill all reported McArthur River mineral reserves and resources, with additional capacity to toll mill ore from new discoveries or other regional deposits.
Waste rock management
At the McArthur
River Operation, mined rock from underground activities is classified as clean waste,
low-grade
mineralized material, or potentially acid generating waste, and transported
on-site
to its appropriate storage location.
Low-grade

low-grade

At Key Lake, there are two stockpiles of mineralized waste rock generated from the historical mining activities of Key Lake: The Deilmann special waste
and the Gaertner special waste stockpiles. Both stockpiles are stored on above ground lined pads and are also presently being used for blending with McArthur River ore. It is anticipated that all of the waste will be removed and consumed as blend
material to manage the mill head grade before the Key Lake site is decommissioned in future.
Effluent treatment and discharge
Treated effluent from the McArthur River Operation is discharged to the Read Creek watershed, via a constructed conveyance channel. Read Creek passes
eastward through Lucy and Unknown Lakes, before discharging into May Creek. May Creek continues flowing eastward through Little Yalowega Lake and then joins Yalowega Lake.
Treated effluent from the Key Lake Operation is discharged to Wolf Lake and flows through the David Creek system. The David Creek system from the
effluent discharge location, in order, consists of: Wolf Lake, Fox Lake, Yak Creek, David Creek, Unknown Lake, Pyrite Creek and Delta Lake. Delta Lake discharges into the

Environmental effects monitoring
Comprehensive environmental monitoring programs are in place at the McArthur River and Key Lake Operations to determine the full extent and nature of any

In November 2017, Cameco and Orano announced production from the McArthur River and Key Lake Operations would be temporarily suspended by the end of

24.3
Mining and milling risks
Production at McArthur River/Key Lake poses many challenges. These challenges include ground water control and management, weak rock formations,
radiation protection, environmental protection, performance of the water treatment systems, mine area transitioning, regulatory approvals, surface and underground fires, equipment reliability and other mining related challenges. Operational
experience gained since the start of production has resulted in a significant reduction in risk.
Water inflows
The consequences of another water inflow at McArthur River would depend on its magnitude, location and timing, but could include a significant
interruption or reduction in production, a material increase in costs, or a loss of mineral reserves. Cameco takes the following steps to reduce the risk of water inflows, but there is no guarantee that these will be successful:
•
Mine design
: Cameco plans for its mine development to take place at safe offset distances from known groundwater
sources whenever possible and practical. In addition, Cameco assesses all planned mine development for relative risk and applies extensive additional technical and operating controls for all higher risk development.
•
Probe and grouting
: Probe and grout drilling is carried out ahead of planned development to identify and grout off
potential water sources.
•
Ground freezing
: Before mining, Cameco drills freeze holes and freezes the ground to form an impermeable barrier
around the area being mined. Ground freezing significantly reduces but does not fully eliminate the risk of water inflows.
•
Pumping capacity and treatment limits
: Cameco’s standard for this mine is to secure pumping capacity of at
least one and a half times the estimated reasonable worst case water inflow. Cameco reviews its dewatering system and requirements at least once a year and before beginning work on any new zone. Cameco believes it has sufficient pumping, water
treatment and surface storage capacity to handle the estimated reasonable worst case sustained inflow.
Transition to new
mine areas
To successfully achieve the assumed production schedule, Cameco must continue to successfully transition into new mining areas,
which includes mine development, ground freezing and investment in critical support infrastructure.
Failure to successfully transition to a new mine
area as scheduled could delay or reduce production, which could have a material and adverse effect on Cameco’s earnings, cash flows and financial condition.
Tunnel rehabilitation
The McArthur
River mine is now over 26 years old and ground support in the older mine areas has been subjected to corrosion and/or ground stresses induced by artificial ground freezing. The site maintains a robust rehabilitation program; however, parts of the
mine are significantly more challenging to rehab due to installed infrastructure (pipes, cable, ducting, etc.). These more challenging areas to rehabilitate require significantly more time to repair and can potentially impact production operations
due to temporary loss of access or services.
Additional resources have been allocated to ground rehabilitation activities to ensure timely rehab
occurs; however, production interruption risks remain.

Accordingly, some residual metallurgical and operational risk remains associated with sustained operation at rates approaching the licensed annual production capacity.
A significant risk to the McArthur River Operation is production interruption from water inflows. Cameco takes the following steps to reduce the risk of
inflows:
Ground freezing:
Before mining, Cameco drills freezeholes and freezes the ground to form an impermeable freeze barrier around the
area being mined. Ground freezing reduces, but does not fully eliminate, the risk of water inflows.
Mine development:
Cameco plans for mine
development to take place away from known groundwater sources whenever possible. In addition, Cameco assesses all planned mine development for relative risk and applies additional technical and operating controls for all higher risk development.
Pumping capacity and treatment limits:
Cameco’s standard is to secure pumping and treatment capacity of at least one and a half times
the estimated maximum sustained inflow. Cameco believes it has sufficient pumping, water treatment and surface storage capacity to handle the estimated maximum sustained inflow. Cameco reviews its dewatering system and requirements at least once a
year and before beginning work on any new zone.
Cameco has demonstrated that the challenging conditions associated with mining the McArthur River
mineral reserves can be managed. Operational experience gained since the start of commercial production has resulted in a significant reduction in risk.

Recommendations
The McArthur River mine/Key Lake mill are mature operations, having packaged approximately 383 million pounds since 1999, and continue to supply a
significant volume of uranium to world markets. To realize the economic benefits from these operations and to mitigate risk, the authors of this technical report make the following recommendations:
Metallurgical processing
It is
recommended that a mineralogical and metallurgical analysis be completed on future mining areas to validate the forecast metallurgical and environmental performance assumptions.
Mineral resources and reserves
The
McArthur River Operation estimated mineral reserves have proven, thus far, to be slightly conservative with more pounds of U
3
O
8
and
tonnage extracted than predicted. Over the years, the mineral reserve model has been calibrated as required to better predict actual production results. Continued review of mineral resource models, the applied mining recovery factor (additional
discussion below), other reserve modifying factors and monitoring of production data against model expectations is recommended as additional data is collected.
Given the size of the current proven and probable mineral reserve inventory and the forecast rate of production, the authors of this technical report are
of the opinion that the current exploration plan to delineate Zones A, McA South 1, McA North 3 and McA North 4 cannot be deferred but is sufficient and does not require acceleration.
Mine recovery
McArthur River assumes
ore recoveries of 97.5% for raisebore mining and 99.0% for drill and blast stope mining which may be considered high and has not been fully validated against actual recoveries of mined out areas.
It is recommended that recovery assumptions be further assessed, documented and peer-reviewed plus recommendations made prior to the next
life-of-mine
update. Any potential assumption changes in recovery are not expected to be significant but may result in a slight reduction in mineral reserves.
Continuous improvement plan
The mine
plan presented in this report assumes that continuous improvement and optimization continue to occur at the McArthur River Mine site. Rates and assumptions assumed are considered reasonably achievable, however, current operational performance is not
at these levels. Areas that are recommended for specific improvement focus are:
•
Long-hole drill and blast cycle improvement
•
Concrete delivery improvement including two product delivery to support equal prioritization of development and production
activities
•
Development productivity improvement
•
Freeze drilling productivity improvement
•
Ventilation management improvement including full implementation and use of
vent-on-demand

could not be validated. At higher production rates, plant and system availability becomes more critical, and production constraints may occur if actual availability is less than what was assumed. It is recommended that plant and system availability
be formally tracked and monitored against the schedule assumptions in the following areas:
